## 1. Glints and glitter: the literature, its real-time descendants, engine support, public code, WebGPU/WebGL2 feasibility, parameters, and fitting to real glitter fabric

### Takeaway
Real-time glitter is a solved-enough problem in 2026: the procedural "count the flakes in the pixel footprint" family (Zirr–Kaplanyan 2016 → Chermain 2020/2021 → Deliot–Belcour 2023 → Kneiphof–Klein 2024/2025 for area lights and environment maps → Kemppinen et al. 2025 constant-time, importance-sampleable, coloured flakes) converges to an ordinary GGX/Beckmann highlight at distance and resolves individual flakes up close, costs roughly 0.2–1.1 ms extra per light at 1080p on a 2018-class discrete GPU, and has public shader code for every step. The only shipping-engine implementation I could confirm is Unreal Engine's Substrate "Glints" (opt-in cvar, Adaptive GBuffer only); Unity has research forks of HDRP but nothing documented as a product feature.

### Cited Findings

**Offline foundations (not real-time; they define the target look)**
- Jakob, Hašan, Yan, Lawrence, Ramamoorthi, Marschner, "Discrete Stochastic Microfacet Models", ACM TOG 33(4) (SIGGRAPH 2014), 115:1–115:10, DOI 10.1145/2601097.2601186: replaces the continuous microfacet distribution with a discrete distribution of scattering particles (i.e. a finite number of mirror flakes counted inside the pixel footprint and a cone of directions) — [EPFL RGL project page](https://rgl.epfl.ch/publications/Jakob2014Discrete)
- Yan, Hašan, Jakob, Lawrence, Marschner, Ramamoorthi, "Rendering Glints on High-Resolution Normal-Mapped Specular Surfaces", ACM TOG 33(4) (SIGGRAPH 2014): explicitly integrates the normal distribution of a high-resolution normal map over the footprint (the "P-NDF"); accurate but hard to accelerate — [paper PDF, UCSD](https://cseweb.ucsd.edu/~ravir/glints.pdf)
- Yan, Hašan, Marschner, Ramamoorthi, "Position-normal distributions for efficient rendering of specular microstructure", ACM TOG 35(4), 2016 — [Crossref record / DOI 10.1145/2897824.2925915](https://doi.org/10.1145/2897824.2925915); later work characterises it as accelerating P-NDF evaluation with a 4D mixture of Gaussians — [Wu et al. 2025, arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.08985v1)
- Further offline/production lineage confirmed via the reference list of Kneiphof & Klein 2025: Atanasov & Koylazov, "A practical stochastic algorithm for rendering mirror-like flakes" (SIGGRAPH 2016 Talks); Atanasov, Wilkie, Koylazov, Křivánek, "A multiscale microfacet model based on inverse bin mapping" (CGF 40, 2021); Chermain, Claux, Mérillou, "Glint rendering based on a multiple-scattering patch BRDF" (CGF 38, 2019); Chermain, Sauvage, Dischler, Dachsbacher, "Importance sampling of glittering BSDFs based on finite mixture distributions" (EGSR 2021); Gamboa, Guertin, Nowrouzezahrai, "Scalable appearance filtering for complex lighting effects" (ACM TOG 37(6), 2018); Deng et al., "Constant-cost spatio-angular prefiltering of glinty appearance using tensor decomposition" (ACM TOG 41(2), 2022); Fan et al., "Efficient specular glints rendering with differentiable regularization" (IEEE TVCG 29(6), 2022) — [Kneiphof & Klein 2025, arXiv HTML reference list](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.02674v1)
- Survey: Zhu, Zhao, Xu, Meng, Wang, Yan, "Recent Advances in Glinty Appearance Rendering", Computational Visual Media 8(4):535–552, 2022, DOI 10.1007/s41095-022-0280-x; defines glinty appearance from microfacet theory, classifies work by representation and by practical rendering method, and benchmarks typical methods on one platform for visuals, speed and memory — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s41095-022-0280-x); [author PDF](https://sites.cs.ucsb.edu/~lingqi/publications/paper_glints_survey.pdf)

**Real-time procedural methods (peer-reviewed unless noted)**
- Bowles & Wang (Studio Gobo), "Sparkly but not too Sparkly! A Stable and Robust Procedural Sparkle Effect", SIGGRAPH 2015 Advances in Real-Time Rendering in Games course (course talk, not peer-reviewed): analyses the aliasing sources of a procedural grid-based sparkle and how to keep it stable at any depth; described as a snow sparkle effect for a AAA console title; slides and a RenderMonkey shader example are on the course page — [Advances in RTR 2015 course page](https://advances.realtimerendering.com/s2015/index.html)
- Wang & Bowles, "A Robust and Flexible Real-Time Sparkle Effect", EGSR 2016 Experimental Ideas & Implementations, pp. 49–54: sparkles come from intersecting a jittered 3D (world-space) grid of sparkle seed points with the rendered surface; integrated into a video game; changes sparkle shape and adds a depth-dependent sparkle scale to reduce aliasing — [HAL record](https://hal.archives-ouvertes.fr/hal-01327604); [EG digital library PDF](https://diglib.eg.org/bitstream/handle/10.2312/sre20161209/049-054.pdf)
- Zirr & Kaplanyan (NVIDIA/KIT), "Real-time Rendering of Procedural Multiscale Materials", I3D 2016, pp. 139–148, DOI 10.1145/2856400.2856409: stable shading of sub-pixel glints and anisotropic microdetails using a biscale NDF and a hierarchy of scales with pixel-footprint estimation at several levels of detail; artist control over global appearance and individual microdetail shape. Public code (text file) and a Shadertoy are linked — [NVIDIA Research page](https://research.nvidia.com/publication/2016-02_real-time-rendering-procedural-multiscale-materials); [Shadertoy ldVGRh](https://www.shadertoy.com/view/ldVGRh); [shader code](http://alphanew.net/releases/glints/shading.txt)
- Chermain, Sauvage, Dischler, Dachsbacher, "Procedural Physically based BRDF for Real-Time Rendering of Glints", Computer Graphics Forum 39(7):243–253 (Pacific Graphics 2020), DOI 10.1111/cgf.14141: uses a precomputed multiscale dictionary of 1D marginal distributions that is roughness-independent and under 1 MiB — [EG digital library](https://diglib.eg.org/handle/10.1111/cgf14141). Reference implementation is OpenGL/GLSL (tested GL 4.1–4.6), MIT-licensed, loads the dictionary as an array texture, supports real-time editing of BRDF parameters, and links a WebGL port by Sylvain Thery — [GitHub ASTex-ICube/real_time_glint](https://github.com/ASTex-ICube/real_time_glint); [WebGL demo](http://igg.unistra.fr/People/reproctex/Demos/Real_Time_Glint/); [dictionary generator](https://github.com/ASTex-ICube/real_time_glint_dictgenerator)
- Chermain, Lucas, Sauvage, Dischler, Dachsbacher, "Real-Time Geometric Glint Anti-Aliasing with Normal Map Filtering", PACM CGIT 4(1) (I3D 2021), DOI 10.1145/3451257: first real-time geometric glint anti-aliasing; filters slope distributions on the fly with GPU texture filtering, adds a slope-correlation factor so the glint BRDF works with LEAN-mapping normal-map filtering, and removes most of the inconsistent appearing/disappearing of glints on strongly curved surfaces in animation — [paper PDF](https://publis.icube.unistra.fr/docs/15022/Chermain2021RealTime.pdf)
- Wang, Deng, Holzschuch, "Real-time glints rendering with pre-filtered discrete stochastic microfacets", CGF 39:144–154, 2020; Tan et al., "Real-time microstructure rendering with MIP-mapped normal map samples", CGF 41:495–506, 2022 (normal-map based, real-time) — [Kneiphof & Klein 2025 reference list](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.02674v1)
- Deliot & Belcour (Unity), "Real-Time Rendering of Glinty Appearances using Distributed Binomial Laws on Anisotropic Grids", Computer Graphics Forum 42(8), e14866 (HPG 2023), arXiv 2306.05051; won the 1st-place Wolfgang Straßer best paper award at HPG 2023 — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05051); [EG digital library](https://diglib.eg.org/handle/10.1111/cgf14866); [author blog](https://thomasdeliot.wixsite.com/blog/single-post/hpg23-real-time-rendering-of-glinty-appearance-using-distributed-binomial-laws-on-anisotropic-grids)
  - Method: project the pixel footprint ellipse to UV space; pick grid cells from discrete scales, anisotropy ratios and orientations (a texture-space grid that emulates anisotropic filtering); per sample, draw the number of reflecting flakes from a binomial law using a loop-free "gated Gaussian" approximation (two random numbers); seeds live on spatial and angular grids and are interpolated to give each glint a lifespan; LODs are blended by interpolating the binomial parameters (not the outputs) to avoid ghosting. 48 binomial evaluations per pixel; a small random-number texture generated once at start-up — [ar5iv HTML of the paper](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2306.05051)
  - Statistics: the expected count equals the smooth NDF D(h) and the variance falls as p(1−p)/N, so the appearance converges to the ordinary microfacet highlight as the number of flakes per pixel grows — [ar5iv HTML](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2306.05051)
  - Cost (RTX 2080, 1920×1080, Unity built-in forward renderer, full-screen test scenes; baseline without glints ≈0.49–0.53 ms): 0.67–1.63 ms for their method vs 0.93–5.03 ms for Zirr–Kaplanyan 2016 and 1.01–5.90 ms for Chermain 2020 (e.g. Sponza 1.32–1.38 vs 3.84–5.03 vs 5.06–5.90 ms); claimed 1.5–5× faster with stable frame time across view angles and densities — [ar5iv HTML](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2306.05051)
  - Stated limitations: punctual lights only (no area lights or image-based lighting); cost linear in the number of lights; not energy-conserving (would fail a white-furnace test) and not importance-sampleable; no anisotropic NDF; quality depends on the UV layout and UV discontinuities cause artefacts — [ar5iv HTML](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2306.05051)
  - Code: standalone source, an executable benchmark and an HDRP teaser demo are distributed as Google Drive downloads from the project page (no licence stated) — [project page](https://ggx-research.github.io/publication/2023/06/09/publication-glints.html)

**2024–2026 follow-ups**
- Kneiphof & Klein (Univ. Bonn), "Real-Time Rendering of Glints in the Presence of Area Lights", Pacific Graphics 2024 Conference Papers, arXiv 2408.13611 (v2 Apr 2025): combines linearly transformed cosines (LTC) for the light integral with the binomial counting model, treating Deliot–Belcour as a black box and replacing its heuristic success probability with one that accounts for light size; a light opening angle γ for point/directional lights replaces the earlier ad-hoc ratio parameter; timings reported at 3840×2160 on an RTX 4090 and a 35 W-limited RTX 4070 Mobile with only a small overhead over Deliot–Belcour. Limitations: artefacts when the per-flake probability exceeds 1 (large lights + low roughness; clamped to the continuous BRDF), no textured lights, no light visibility, iridescence and IBL left as future work — [arXiv HTML v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.13611v2); [EG digital library](https://diglib.eg.org/handle/10.2312/pg20241293)
  - Why earlier point-light glint shaders are wrong for real lights: for an infinitesimal light and perfectly mirror-like flakes the probability that a flake reflects exactly toward the eye is zero, so earlier methods relied on a hand-tuned heuristic that ignored light size — [arXiv HTML v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.13611v2)
  - Code: Unity project with a modified HDRP 14.0.10 `HDRP/Lit` (files `Runtime/Material/Glints/Glints2023.hlsl`, `Glints2024.hlsl`, `GlintsSubdivision.hlsl`) — [GitHub tomix1024/AreaLightGlintsUnityProject](https://github.com/tomix1024/AreaLightGlintsUnityProject)
- Kneiphof & Klein, "Real-time Image-based Lighting of Glints", Computer Graphics Forum 2025 (EGSR 2025), DOI 10.1111/cgf.70175, arXiv 2507.02674: fully dynamic materials and environment maps; the environment map is partitioned into a few regions of roughly constant radiance (K = 4 or 8 radiance levels spaced in log space), the regions' indicator functions are prefiltered with the NDF using standard environment-map filtering, and at shading time the probabilities drive hierarchical multinomial sampling with a new dual-gated Gaussian approximation; overhead is small relative to one directional light; the prefiltered environment map needs twice the memory of the smooth case (16-bit channels suffice, 8-bit do not); per-frame prefiltering is feasible. Limitations: only brightness is modelled (nearby coloured lights give white glints), no occlusion, single scattering, isotropic GGX only, glint angular lifetime not modelled — [arXiv abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.02674); [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.02674v1); [Wiley/CGF](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/cgf.70175)
  - Code and precompiled Windows/Linux demos (modified HDRP 14.0.10; IBL plus improved area/directional light code) — [GitHub tomix1024/IBLGlints-Demo](https://github.com/tomix1024/IBLGlints-Demo)
- Kemppinen, Paulin, Thonat, Thiery, Lehtinen, Boubekeur (Adobe Research / Aalto / Télécom Paris), "Evaluating and Sampling Glinty NDFs in Constant Time", ACM TOG 44(6) (SIGGRAPH Asia 2025), DOI 10.1145/3763282: models facets as a 4D point process on an implicit multiscale grid so the facets most likely to produce a highlight are found directly; facet normals follow a target GGX or Beckmann NDF to which the model converges with increasing surface area; no memory or precomputation; supports importance sampling, anisotropic appearance and individually coloured particles; a base implementation is released as a standalone fragment shader — [project page](https://perso.telecom-paristech.fr/boubek/papers/Glinty/); [Adobe Research](https://research.adobe.com/publication/glinty/); [Shadertoy tcdGDl](https://www.shadertoy.com/view/tcdGDl)
- Shah, Gamboa, Gruson, Narayanan, "Neural Histogram-Based Glint Rendering of Surfaces With Spatially Varying Roughness", CGF 43(4) (EGSR 2024), DOI 10.1111/cgf.15157: a neural histogram returns per-pixel NDFs for arbitrary footprint positions/sizes on normal-mapped surfaces, supports spatially varying and live-editable roughness, and integrates direct illumination for all light directions in constant time — [EG digital library](https://diglib.eg.org/handle/10.1111/cgf15157); [project page](https://ishaanshah.xyz/neural_glint/)
- Xing, Tan, Xu, Wang, "A Tiny Example Based Procedural Model for Real-Time Glinty Appearance Rendering", J. Computer Science and Technology 39(4):771–784, 2024: builds microstructure from small example patterns with NDFs precomputed as 4D Gaussians in multi-scale NDF maps; GPU-friendly, low memory — [JCST](https://jcst.ict.ac.cn/en/article/doi/10.1007/s11390-024-4123-3)
- Wu, Luan, Hašan, Ramamoorthi, "Position-Normal Manifold for Efficient Glint Rendering on High-Resolution Normal Maps", arXiv 2505.08985 (May 2025): reduces the glint NDF integral to a mesh-intersection problem with a clustering hierarchy; reports an order of magnitude speed-up over baselines (real-time status not stated on the arXiv page) — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.08985)
- Zhao, Wang, Wang, Holzschuch, "A discrete microfacet model for transparent glints rendering", Computational Visual Media 11(5):953–963, 2025: transparent (refractive) glints such as frost, arbitrary NDFs, converges to the smooth model at high particle counts — [SciOpen](https://www.sciopen.com/article/10.26599/CVM.2025.9450396)

### Inferences
- **Recommended glitter approach.** Use a Deliot–Belcour-style binomial-counting glint lobe as the specular term of glitter fabrics, with the Kneiphof–Klein 2024 area-light probability (so the light's angular size, not a fudge factor, sets sparkle frequency) and, if the environment map must sparkle too, the Kneiphof–Klein 2025 radiance-level prefilter. Evaluate Kemppinen et al. 2025 as the forward-looking alternative: it removes the noise texture, is constant-time, importance-sampleable and natively supports per-flake colour — exactly what coloured/holographic leotard glitter needs — and ships as a single fragment shader.
- **WebGL2/WebGPU feasibility.** All of these are plain fragment-shader techniques using screen-space derivatives, integer hashing and (for some) one small lookup texture. Direct evidence: Zirr–Kaplanyan and Kemppinen et al. run on Shadertoy (WebGL2-class GLSL), and Chermain 2020 has a WebGL port. The Unity HLSL (`Glints2023/2024.hlsl`) is the best porting source for WGSL/TSL. Nothing requires compute shaders or ray tracing. The extra requirements are an LTC lookup texture pair for area lights and a second set of prefiltered environment-map channels for IBL glints.
- **Cost expectation.** From the published table, glints add about 0.2–1.1 ms per light at 1080p when the glinty surface fills the screen on an RTX 2080. A leotard typically covers a fraction of the frame in full-body view, and fills it only in close-ups; an integrated GPU roughly an order of magnitude slower than that card should still hold 60 fps with one to three sparkle-casting lights if the glint lobe is restricted to glitter materials. This is an estimate that needs measurement on target hardware.
- **Parameters to expose.** (1) flake density (flakes per mm², stored as log density as Unreal does); (2) macro roughness α of the target NDF = spread of flake tilt angles (controls how wide the sparkling band around the highlight is); (3) per-flake roughness / light angular size (controls how often and how long a flake fires); (4) UV scale tying texture space to millimetres; (5) flake colour distribution. Because fashion patterns are flat 2D pieces, pattern-space coordinates in millimetres are a natural, distortion-free UV set for the glint grid, which also sidesteps the method's sensitivity to distorted UVs (seams between pattern pieces remain discontinuities but coincide with real seams).
- **Fitting to a real glitter fabric.** Flake density and size can be counted from a calibrated macro photograph (flakes per mm²; mean flake diameter gives coverage). The macro roughness can be fitted from the angular width of the sparkling band in a photo series under a small light (or from a handheld gonio series), and the per-flake term from the fraction of flakes lit at the specular angle. A practical loop is to photograph the swatch under one small light at two or three angles, compute sparkle statistics (count of bright spots per cm², their intensity histogram) and tune density/α until the rendered statistics match (see section 8).
- Unreal's parameterisation (log micro-facet density, UV-space glints, opt-in, most expensive tier) is consistent with a Deliot–Belcour-type implementation, but I could not open Epic's slides or source to confirm which paper it follows.

## 2. Coloured and holographic glitter; car-paint flake shaders as an analogue

### Takeaway
Per-flake colour is now a first-class feature (Kemppinen et al. 2025), thin-film iridescence has a standard real-time model (Belcour & Barla 2017, adopted by Unity HDRP, glTF and three.js), and automotive rendering supplies the mature precedent for sparkling flakes in an engine (measured flake BTFs in Unity HDRP's AxF car-paint shader, AxF import into Unreal Substrate in UE 5.8). True holographic (diffraction-grating) glitter has peer-reviewed models (Stam 1999; Toisoul & Ghosh 2017) but no confirmed combination with a real-time glint BRDF.

### Cited Findings
- Kemppinen et al. 2025 explicitly covers individually coloured particles and anisotropic appearance, with per-facet colour control shown on a Beckmann variant — [project page](https://perso.telecom-paristech.fr/boubek/papers/Glinty/)
- Kneiphof & Klein 2025's IBL glints model only brightness: coloured light sources that are close together produce white glints (a stated limitation) — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.02674v1); their 2024 area-light paper lists the interaction of glints with iridescence as future work — [arXiv HTML v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.13611v2)
- Belcour & Barla, "A Practical Extension to Microfacet Theory for the Modeling of Varying Iridescence", ACM TOG 36(4) (SIGGRAPH 2017): an anti-aliased (spectrally pre-integrated) thin-film Fresnel term inside microfacet theory, integrated in a real-time engine and used in Unity's HD render pipeline; supplemental code, slides and community Unity/Unreal/Blender ports are linked — [author page](https://belcour.github.io/blog/research/publication/2017/05/01/brdf-thin-film.html)

### Inferences
- **Coloured glitter**: assign each flake a colour from a seeded distribution (hue/saturation variation as CLO exposes, or a discrete palette for multi-colour "confetti" glitter). With a counting model this means splitting the per-pixel flake count into colour classes (a multinomial split) or, more simply, hashing a colour per grid cell; Kemppinen et al. is the only verified method that does this by construction.
- **Iridescent ("AB"/"mystique") flakes**: multiply each flake's Fresnel term by the Belcour–Barla thin-film reflectance evaluated at the flake's half-vector angle, with a per-flake thickness jitter (tens of nanometres) so neighbouring flakes show different hues. The cost is one extra thin-film evaluation per glint lobe.
- **Holographic flakes** are diffraction gratings, not thin films: colour depends on grating period and orientation and the light/view geometry (first-order condition d·(sin θi + sin θo) = mλ). A workable real-time model is a per-flake random grating orientation plus a 1D spectral lookup (wavelength → RGB) evaluated from the grating equation — this gives the characteristic hard rainbow flashes that sweep through the spectrum as the garment moves. This construction is my own synthesis of Stam-style diffraction with a discrete flake model; I found no paper that validates it for glitter.
- **Car paint as analogue**: the automotive pipeline shows two viable representations — procedural statistics (the glint BRDFs above) and measured flake BTF slices (AxF). For a leotard tool the procedural route is preferable because designers need continuous control over flake size/density/colour, and AxF-style measured slices cannot be edited or authored (Unity's own documentation notes this).
- Because IBL glints (2025) lose light colour, coloured show lighting should be represented as explicit analytic/area lights for the sparkle term, keeping the environment map for the smooth lobe.

### Gaps
- Not verified: any peer-reviewed real-time method combining diffraction-grating (holographic) flakes with a glint BRDF; wave-optics glint work I recall (Yan et al. 2018 wave-optics microgeometry; Werner et al. 2017 and Velinov et al. 2018 on scratch iridescence; Guillén et al. 2020 pearlescent framework) could not be re-confirmed in-session and is not cited as fact.

## 3. Sequins: geometry, how they sit on fabric, real-time approaches, distance filtering, and published production work

### Takeaway
I found no peer-reviewed or studio publication dedicated to sequin rendering that I could verify in this session; the defensible design is to treat a sequin as a very large, slightly rough flake: instanced tilted discs up close, handing off to a glint BRDF whose discrete normal set is the same per-sequin tilts. The glint literature guarantees the far-field limit (a microfacet highlight whose width equals the tilt spread), which is what makes the hand-off consistent.

### Cited Findings
- Sequins are small, generally disc-shaped shiny ornaments; in costuming a sequin has a centre hole while a spangle has its hole at the top; paillettes are typically very large and flat; some sequins are faceted to increase reflectivity; they can be stitched flat (still, less likely to fall off) or at a single point so they dangle and catch more light; materials moved from metal to gelatin to plastics (vinyl) — [Wikipedia: Sequin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sequin)
- Rhinestone material is often used in place of sequins on costumes — [Wikipedia: Rhinestone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhinestone)
- The far-field consistency property needed for a sequin LOD hand-off is established for glint models: Deliot & Belcour's expected flake count equals the smooth NDF with variance p(1−p)/N — [ar5iv HTML](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2306.05051); Kemppinen et al.'s facet normals follow a chosen GGX/Beckmann NDF to which the model converges with increasing area — [project page](https://perso.telecom-paristech.fr/boubek/papers/Glinty/)
- Glints on curved, animated surfaces flicker unless the glint model is filtered for curvature; Chermain et al. 2021 is the method that addresses this (slope-correlation factor + LEAN-style filtering) — [paper PDF](https://publis.icube.unistra.fr/docs/15022/Chermain2021RealTime.pdf)
- Real-time glint research is already demonstrated on garments: Kneiphof & Klein 2025 credit a "Walking Dress" model among their test assets — [arXiv HTML, acknowledgments](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.02674v1)
- The only studio publications on garment embellishment I could confirm are Disney Animation's curve-based procedural embroidery talks: "Making Beautiful Embroidery for Frozen 2" (Liu, Wright, Alvarado; SIGGRAPH 2020 Talks) and "Embroidery and Cloth Fiber Workflows on Disney's Encanto" (Velasquez, Alvarado, Liu, Simmons; SIGGRAPH 2022 Talks, DOI 10.1145/3532836.3536250); neither page mentions sequins or beads — [Disney Animation: Frozen 2 embroidery](https://www.disneyanimation.com/publications/making-beautiful-embroidery-for-frozen-2/); [Disney Animation: Encanto embroidery](https://www.disneyanimation.com/publications/embroidery-and-cloth-fiber-workflows-on-disney-s-encanto/); Disney's publication index shows no title containing sequin, glitter, glint, sparkle, crystal, bead or gem — [Disney Animation publications](https://disneyanimation.com/publications/)
- Production-renderer precedent for mirror-like flakes (offline): Atanasov & Koylazov, "A practical stochastic algorithm for rendering mirror-like flakes", SIGGRAPH 2016 Talks — [reference list](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.02674v1)
- Fashion CAD has no dedicated sequin tool that I could find: a scan of all 962 English article titles in CLO's help center on 13 Oct 2026 returned none containing sequin, bead, rhinestone, hotfix, crystal, spangle or embellish (the closest features are OBJ trims, graphics and the render-only glitter material) — [CLO Help Center](https://support.clo3d.com/hc/en-us); a scan of 965 Browzwear help-article titles likewise found none (closest: 3D/2.5D trims and Substance artwork presets) — [Browzwear Help Center](https://help.browzwear.com/en/); the Style3D Studio help sections I browsed (Home, Assets, Tools; V10.0 of July 2026 listed) contain no rhinestone/sequin entries — [Style3D Studio Help](https://help.style3d.com/studio/en/)

### Inferences
- **Geometry LODs (proposed).** Flat disc with hole: a 12–16-gon annulus (24–32 triangles) or a single alpha-tested quad; cupped sequin: add one ring of vertices (concave normal variation that gives a moving hot-spot); faceted sequin: 6–8 flat facets. Flat hotfix spangles are bonded flat, so their normal equals the fabric normal plus a small random tilt (a degree or two, from adhesive and fabric texture); sewn/hanging sequins pivot about the stitch and need a larger per-instance tilt (and in a posed/animated garment a gravity- and motion-dependent tilt). Overlapping fish-scale rows are produced by placing rows at less than one diameter pitch with a consistent tilt about the row axis so each sequin rests on the row below; this needs ordinary depth testing (opaque discs), not blending.
- **Twinkle comes from tilt variance.** A sequin is a near-mirror several millimetres wide. It fires when its normal lines up with the half-vector within the light's angular radius. The per-instance tilt distribution (direction and magnitude, seeded per sequin) is therefore the single most important appearance parameter; expose it as "tilt spread" in degrees.
- **Three real-time representations.** (1) Instanced geometry with per-instance variation (transform, tilt, colour, seed) — best for close-ups and for individually placed/editable sequins; (2) procedural texture-space sequins (cell lookup in pattern UV space with a per-cell tilt normal and disc mask) — one material, zero instances, ideal for all-over sequin fabric but not individually editable; (3) point/quad impostors with an analytic per-sequin specular evaluation for the mid-range.
- **Distance hand-off.** When a sequin projects to less than about two pixels, replace geometry by a glint lobe evaluated in the fabric shader with flake density = sequins per mm², macro roughness = the tilt spread, and per-flake roughness = the sequin's own roughness, using the same per-sequin seeds so the same sequins fire before and after the switch. For regular layouts the count per footprint is deterministic (footprint area ÷ pitch²), which is easier than random glitter. Diffuse/body colour should be averaged the same way (coverage-weighted) so the garment does not change brightness across the transition.
- **Count sanity check.** Assuming roughly 0.6–0.9 m² of garment surface (my assumption from body-surface proportions, not a sourced figure), a full-coverage 4 mm-pitch sequin layout is on the order of 40–60 thousand sequins, about twice that with half-overlapped rows — well within instancing range if distant sequins are impostors.

- No verified film/game production talk or paper specifically on sequins. I recall (unverified, not to be cited) that sequined/glitter characters in animated features (e.g. DreamWorks' "Trolls" glitter, Disney's "Zootopia" pop-star dress) had bespoke shading work; DreamWorks' research site was under maintenance, Pixar's library index did not expose titles, and without search I could not confirm any title, author or venue.

## 4. Rhinestones/crystals: geometry and optics of flat-back stones, real-time gem methods, dispersion, AB coating, per-stone cost and viability at 10,000+

### Takeaway
A flat-back rhinestone is optically much simpler than a diamond: a low-index (about 1.5–1.7) glass dome of 8–18 facets plus a table over an opaque silver mirror, so light enters through the crown, reflects once off the foil and leaves through the crown. Per-pixel ray tracing inside the stone (the three-mesh-bvh/drei/iJewel approach) is proven in browsers for hundreds of gems but is only needed in close-up; at 10,000+ stones the viable design is an LOD chain in which distant stones are evaluated from a small precomputed per-cut table of exit lobes rather than traced.

### Cited Findings

**Physical make-up**
- Rhinestones are diamond simulants made from crystal (lead) glass or polymers such as acrylic; Strass coated the lower side of lead glass with metal powder, later replaced by foil and vapour-deposited metal coatings; Swarovski's "Aurora Borealis" glass dates from 1956; AB stones carry an iridescent coating applied by vacuum metal deposition; hotfix stones have a heat-activated glue on the flat bottom (about 180–200 °C); sizes use the "ss" scale (8ss ≈ 2.3 mm, 10ss ≈ 2.8 mm); Swarovski (Austria) and Preciosa (Czech Republic) are the main makers — [Wikipedia: Rhinestone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhinestone)
- Ordinary glass has a refractive index of about 1.5; adding lead raises it to roughly 1.7–1.8 and increases dispersion; lead glass is typically 18–40% PbO and "lead crystal" needs at least 24%; modern lead-free crystal substitutes barium, zinc or potassium oxide — [Wikipedia: Lead glass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lead_glass)
- Swarovski crystal has been lead-free since 2012; the Xilion cut was released in 2004 for Roses (flat-backs) and Chatons; coatings include Aurora Borealis (gives the surface a rainbow appearance), Crystal Transmission, Volcano, Aurum, Shimmer and Dorado, some applied to part of the object or twice ("AB 2X") — [Wikipedia: Swarovski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swarovski)
- Preciosa's own comparison sheet (vendor-authored, so the qualitative ratings are marketing) lists facet counts and size ranges: Chaton Rose MAXIMA 12–18 facets, ss2–ss48 (smallest hotfix ss3 with 12 facets); Swarovski Xirius/Xilion/Xero 8–16 facets, ss3–ss48 (8 facets at ss3); Preciosa VIVA 12 with 12 facets, ss5–ss48; Stellux 12 facets, ss6–ss40; about 70 colours and 90 coatings/effects for MAXIMA — [Preciosa MAXIMA benchmark PDF](https://img.preciosacomponents.com/files/landingpage/lp-4/downloads/Preciosa_Chaton_Rose_MAXIMA_Benchmark.pdf)
- Preciosa describes the underside as a layered system: a genuine silver reflective layer plus a protective "DuraFoiling" layer; says the glass composition is engineered for a higher refractive index than many competitors (no value given); and claims up to 264 unique reflected light rays for the MAXIMA flat-back; variants are hotfix, no-hotfix and sew-on — [Preciosa MAXIMA FAQ PDF](https://img.preciosacomponents.com/files/landingpage/lp-4/downloads/Preciosa_Chaton_Rose_MAXIMA_Frequently_Asked_Questions_EN.pdf); [Preciosa flatback stones page](https://www.preciosacomponents.com/c/10325/flatback-stones)
- Cut vocabulary and light-performance terms: table (largest crown facet), crown (above girdle), girdle, pavilion, culet; a round brilliant has 57/58 facets; brilliance = light returned to the viewer (external surface reflection plus internal reflection, which for diamond relies on its 24.4° critical angle), fire = spectral flashes from dispersion with the crown acting as a prism, scintillation = sparkle seen as stone, light or viewer move; a rose cut has a flat base with no pavilion and a faceted dome — [Wikipedia: Diamond cut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diamond_cut)
- Gemological dispersion (nB − nG): diamond 0.044, flint glass 0.041, quartz 0.013; fire also depends on facet angles, polish, lighting and viewing angle — [Wikipedia: Dispersion (optics)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dispersion_(optics))
- Sasian, Quick, Sheffield, Caudill, Yantzer, "Evaluation of brilliance, fire, and scintillation in round brilliant gemstones", Optical Engineering 46(9), 2007: introduces maps for evaluating gemstone illumination effects as a basis for grading — [DOI 10.1117/1.2769018](https://doi.org/10.1117/1.2769018)

**Real-time gem rendering methods**
- Guy & Soler, "Graphics Gems Revisited", ACM TOG (SIGGRAPH 2004): real-time rendering of faceted coloured gemstones on graphics hardware using fragment programs, cube mapping and floating-point rendering, with controlled approximations of the light transport inside the stone, aimed at jewellery prototyping (peer-reviewed) — [INRIA Maverick page](https://maverick.inria.fr/Publications/2004/GS04/)
- Wyman, "An approximate image-space approach for interactive refraction", ACM TOG 24(3):1050–1053 (SIGGRAPH 2005): the peer-reviewed basis for two-interface, image-space refraction tricks — [DOI 10.1145/1073204.1073310](https://doi.org/10.1145/1073204.1073310)
- three-mesh-bvh (MIT) ships a "Gem Refraction Path Tracing" example: a fragment shader refracts the view ray into the mesh, then loops up to `bounces` times (default 3, GUI 1–10) calling `bvhIntersectFirstHit` against a BVH uploaded as textures, reflecting on total internal reflection and exiting otherwise; default IOR 2.4; dispersion by tracing three paths with IOR scaled by (1 ± aberrationStrength) or a "fast chroma" mode that offsets the green ray's exit direction; samples an equirectangular HDR environment; no Fresnel term; hardware antialiasing disabled because interpolated normals at face edges upset the ray casts — [GitHub gkjohnson/three-mesh-bvh](https://github.com/gkjohnson/three-mesh-bvh); [example source diamond.js](https://github.com/gkjohnson/three-mesh-bvh/blob/master/example/diamond.js); [demo](https://gkjohnson.github.io/three-mesh-bvh/diamond.html)
- drei `MeshRefractionMaterial` (credits N8Programs' `N8python/diamonds`, built on three-mesh-bvh's shader BVH structs): props `bounces` (documented default 2; the docs warn higher is expensive), `ior` 2.4, `fresnel`, `aberrationStrength` (can be expensive), `fastChroma` default true, `envMap` (or a CubeCamera to reflect the scene); the vertex shader has a `USE_INSTANCING` path that folds `instanceMatrix` into the object-space ray transform, so instanced gems are supported — [drei docs](https://drei.docs.pmnd.rs/shaders/mesh-refraction-material); [drei source](https://github.com/pmndrs/drei/blob/master/src/materials/MeshRefractionMaterial.tsx); [N8python/diamonds (MIT)](https://github.com/N8python/diamonds)
- iJewel3D / WebGi (commercial jewellery viewer on three.js/Threepipe, WebGL): diamonds use ray-traced refraction and dispersion distinct from PBR materials; parameters include refractive index (default 2.60), dispersion, absorption, reflectivity, ray bounces (4–5 by default, up to 16 suggested for some cuts), environment rotation offsets, gamma, per-material bloom and a thin-film layer; the vendor states sparkle and fire depend heavily on the HDRI and its rotation, and claims hundreds of diamonds render at once, including on mobile — [iJewel3D docs: Diamond Materials](https://docs.ijewel3d.com/playground/diamond-materials.html); [iJewel3D docs: introduction](https://docs.ijewel3d.com/introduction/ijewel.html); [WebGi](https://webgi.dev/)
- Hardware ray tracing is not available to browser apps: WebGPU's documented feature list contains no ray-tracing or acceleration-structure feature — [MDN: GPUSupportedFeatures](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/GPUSupportedFeatures)

**Dispersion ("fire") and thin-film (AB) in real-time material standards**
- glTF `KHR_materials_dispersion` (ratified): dispersion = 20 / Abbe number (diamond V=55 → 0.36; crown glass V=59 → 0.33; polycarbonate V=32 → 0.625); implementations trace transmission separately per colour channel with a per-channel IOR spread around the material IOR — [Khronos extension README](https://github.com/KhronosGroup/glTF/blob/main/extensions/2.0/Khronos/KHR_materials_dispersion/README.md)
- three.js `MeshPhysicalMaterial` has `dispersion` (only on transmissive objects), `ior` 1.0–2.333, `transmission`, `thickness`, attenuation and `iridescence*`; the docs note it is the most expensive built-in material per pixel — [three.js docs](https://threejs.org/docs/pages/MeshPhysicalMaterial.html)
- Thin-film interference has a standard real-time formulation (Belcour & Barla 2017; glTF iridescence; Unreal's Substrate Thin-Film helper node computes F0/F90 from thin-film parameters) — [Belcour & Barla page](https://belcour.github.io/blog/research/publication/2017/05/01/brdf-thin-film.html); [Khronos iridescence README](https://github.com/KhronosGroup/glTF/blob/main/extensions/2.0/Khronos/KHR_materials_iridescence/README.md); [Epic: Overview of Substrate Materials](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/en-us/unreal-engine/overview-of-substrate-materials-in-unreal-engine)
- CLO's "Gem (Render Only)" material is described as glass-like but reflecting light in multiple colours, with absorption and an Abbe value — offline/interactive V-Ray only, not the real-time viewport — [CLO Help: Material Type](https://support.clo3d.com/hc/en-us/articles/115015998728-Material-Type)

### Inferences
- **Optical model of a hotfix flat-back (proposed).** Geometry: flat circular base, a table, and one or two rings of crown facets (8–18 depending on cut and size, per the Preciosa sheet), i.e. a convex polyhedron of roughly 10–20 planes. Because the base is an opaque mirror, the stone can be "unfolded": reflecting the crown about the base plane gives a symmetric convex bi-pyramid of twice the plane count through which light simply refracts in, travels, and refracts out (or totally internally reflects and continues). No BVH is needed: ray-vs-convex-polyhedron is a loop over the planes taking the nearest positive exit distance. At n ≈ 1.5–1.7 the critical angle is about 36–42° (versus 24.4° for diamond), so most rays exit after one or two internal segments — a bounce cap of 3–4 loses little energy, far fewer than the 4–16 bounces jewellery viewers use for diamond.
- **Per-pixel cost (close-up LOD only).** Roughly (planes ≈ 20–36) × (segments ≤ 4) plane tests, times 1 (fast-chroma) or 3 (true per-channel dispersion) — on the order of 100–400 dot products per covered pixel plus two thin-film evaluations if AB-coated and three environment/light lookups. This is cheaper than the BVH traversal the web demos use and its cost is bounded by screen coverage, not stone count: in an extreme close-up a few hundred stones fill the frame.
- **Dispersion setting.** Crystal glass has diamond-class dispersion but a much lower index (flint glass 0.041 vs diamond 0.044 on the gemological scale, per the cited table). In glTF terms, lead-type crystal should sit well above crown glass's 0.33; a dense flint with Abbe number in the 30s would give about 0.55–0.67 (the Abbe figure is a textbook value I did not re-verify). Expose index (1.50–1.70) and dispersion as per-product presets, since manufacturers do not publish them.
- **Why "fire" needs small lights.** Each exit facet acts as a prism that fans white light into a narrow spectrum; a viewer sees a pure colour only if the light source is angularly small enough that the fans from different source points do not overlap back to white, and if the eye/camera aperture samples only part of the fan. Broad soft lighting therefore gives brilliance without fire. This follows from basic prism optics and matches the gemological statements cited; I recall this pupil-clipping argument from the Sasian et al. paper but could only verify its abstract.
- **AB coating model.** Treat AB as a thin-film (interference) layer on the crown surfaces. It modulates the external reflection spectrally and, because what is not reflected is transmitted, tints the light entering and leaving the stone with the complementary colour; with angle-dependent optical path length the hues shift across facets. Implementation: replace the Fresnel term at the crown interface (entry reflection, entry transmission, exit transmission) with a Belcour–Barla evaluation; one film thickness per stone type plus a small per-stone jitter. Partially absorbing metal-oxide coatings would need an absorption factor; no physical thickness/IOR data for commercial AB coatings was found.
- **Precomputed per-cut "exit-lobe" tables for massive instancing (proposed, not from literature).** For each stone type (cut × index × coating), precompute offline or at load time, for a grid of view directions over the hemisphere (e.g. 64×64 octahedral bins), the K strongest outgoing-to-incoming direction mappings (each a mirror-like "virtual facet": incoming direction, projected-area × Fresnel weight, chromatic spread vector). Preciosa's claim of up to 264 distinct reflected rays suggests the right order of magnitude (hundreds of lobes over all views, a dozen or so per view). At run time a mid/far stone is shaded as Σ weight × light(incoming direction) for analytic lights (with a smooth acceptance cone equal to the light's angular radius) plus an environment lookup per lobe — no tracing. Storage is around half a megabyte per stone type at 16 lobes × 8 bytes per bin. The table is independent of lighting, so interactive light changes cost nothing; per-instance rotation just rotates the lookup.
- **Viability at 10,000+ stones.** Tracing every stone is unnecessary and, in browsers, risky (the jewellery vendor's own claim is only in the hundreds of stones). With the LOD chain of section 5, only stones larger than a few tens of pixels are traced; the rest are a few-triangle mesh or a quad shaded from the table. Native hardware ray tracing (DXR/Vulkan) could trace instanced stone BLASes directly but is unavailable in browsers and impractical on integrated GPUs, so it should be an optional quality tier at most.

### Gaps
- No manufacturer data sheet with actual refractive index, Abbe number, facet angles/proportions or AB-coating composition/thickness was retrieved; exact geometry will need supplier CAD models or measurement (Preciosa's MAXIMA page links a "Digital Assets" download that I did not open).
- Could not verify the widely repeated "cube map of back-face normals" gem trick against a canonical source, nor any Unity/Unreal marketplace gem shader, nor whether KeyShot/Blender/Unreal path tracer features (spectral dispersion) are relevant; none are cited.
- Guy & Soler's details (bounce counts, polarisation, performance) were not read beyond the abstract page; the GIA "fire" modelling papers I recall (Hemphill et al. 1998; Reinitz et al. 2001, Gems & Gemology) could not be fetched and are not cited as fact.

## 5. Scale strategy: instancing limits (WebGL2, WebGPU, native), triangle budgets, LOD chain, culling, per-instance layout, in-place updates while painting, GPU picking, memory

### Takeaway
Raw instance count is not the bottleneck: a million instances at 32 bytes each is 32 MB, inside WebGPU's default buffer limits, and a realistic garment physically holds at most about 10^5 stones or sequins — the million-element case is glitter, which belongs in the BRDF, not in instances. What matters is an LOD chain (traced facet mesh → cheap facet mesh → quad impostor → aggregated glint lobe) driven by projected size, GPU-side culling where compute is available, sub-range buffer updates for painting, and an ID-buffer pick.

### Cited Findings
- WebGPU default limits: `maxBufferSize` 256 MB, `maxStorageBufferBindingSize` 128 MB, `maxUniformBufferBindingSize` 64 KB, 8 vertex buffers, 16 vertex attributes, 2048-byte max vertex stride, 8192 max 2D texture size, 256 array layers, 8 storage buffers per stage, 8 colour attachments; higher limits can be requested if the adapter reports them — [MDN: GPUSupportedLimits](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/GPUSupportedLimits)
- WebGPU shipping status (gpuweb wiki): Chromium on Windows/macOS/ChromeOS since Chrome 113, Android (Android 12+ on ARM/Qualcomm/Intel) since Chrome 121, Linux from Chrome 144 (Intel Gen12+) and 147 (NVIDIA on Wayland); Firefox 141 on Windows, 145/147 on macOS, Linux and Android expected during 2026; Safari 26 on macOS/iOS/iPadOS/visionOS — [gpuweb Implementation Status](https://github.com/gpuweb/gpuweb/wiki/Implementation-Status)
- Core WebGPU has single `drawIndirect`/`drawIndexedIndirect` calls; multi-draw indirect exists only as the non-standard `chromium-experimental-multi-draw-indirect` feature behind Chrome's unsafe-WebGPU flag (announced with Chrome 131) — [Chrome: What's new in WebGPU 131](https://developer.chrome.com/blog/new-in-webgpu-131); non-zero `firstInstance` in indirect draws needs the optional `indirect-first-instance` feature — [MDN: GPUSupportedFeatures](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/GPUSupportedFeatures)
- GPU-driven pattern in WebGPU: keep per-instance data in buffers; each frame a compute shader tests visibility, writes surviving instance indices to a visible-instance array and increments the instance count in that object's indirect-draw arguments; the vertex shader looks instances up through the built-in instance index; fully culled objects draw zero instances — [Toji: Render bundle best practices](https://toji.dev/webgpu-best-practices/render-bundles.html)
- Put all indirect draw arguments in one buffer: Chrome/Dawn on D3D12 validates indirect buffers with hidden compute dispatches per buffer — about 3 ms for 412 draws spread across buffers versus about 10 µs when combined — [Toji: Indirect draw best practices](https://toji.dev/webgpu-best-practices/indirect-draws.html)
- Updating buffers: `queue.writeBuffer()` is the recommended default, takes a destination offset (partial updates), and is preferred unless profiling shows a bottleneck; a ring of mapped staging buffers is the advanced option — [Toji: Buffer upload best practices](https://toji.dev/webgpu-best-practices/buffer-uploads.html)
- Why not one draw per element: an unoptimised one-draw-per-object WebGPU sample managed about 8,000 cubes at 75 fps on an M1 Mac and about 15,000 after optimisation; instancing is recommended once hundreds of copies of a model are drawn, and indirect drawing enables GPU-side culling — [WebGPU Fundamentals: Optimization](https://webgpufundamentals.org/webgpu/lessons/webgpu-optimization.html)
- WebGL2: batch into few large draws; effectively all systems support at least 4096 max texture size and 16 vertex attributes; avoid blocking calls (`getError`, synchronous `readPixels`); asynchronous readback uses `readPixels` into a `PIXEL_PACK_BUFFER`, `fenceSync`, polling `clientWaitSync`, then `getBufferSubData`; budget VRAM per screen pixel — [MDN: WebGL best practices](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/WebGL_API/WebGL_best_practices)
- `WEBGL_multi_draw` (WebGL1/2 extension, limited availability) adds multi-draw calls including instanced variants and `gl_DrawID` — [MDN: WEBGL_multi_draw](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/WEBGL_multi_draw)
- three.js: `BatchedMesh` renders many objects sharing one material with multi-draw batching, per-object frustum culling (`perObjectFrustumCulled`), sorting, per-instance matrix/colour/visibility setters, add/delete of instances and an `optimize()` repack; capacities are fixed up front and resizable — [three.js docs: BatchedMesh](https://threejs.org/docs/pages/BatchedMesh.html); `IndirectStorageBufferAttribute` holds indirect draw parameters for compute-driven drawing and works only with `WebGPURenderer` on a WebGPU backend — [three.js docs: IndirectStorageBufferAttribute](https://threejs.org/docs/pages/IndirectStorageBufferAttribute.html)
- Babylon.js thin instances (since v4.2): no per-instance JS objects; a 16-float matrix per instance plus custom registered attributes; bulk `Float32Array` buffers that may be over-allocated; buffers are static unless flagged otherwise and need an explicit "buffer updated" call; all instances are drawn or none (no per-instance culling); picking is supported — [Babylon.js docs: Thin Instances](https://doc.babylonjs.com/features/featuresDeepDive/mesh/copies/thinInstances)
- GPU-driven pipelines (native precedent): Haar & Aaltonen (Ubisoft), "GPU-Driven Rendering Pipelines", SIGGRAPH 2015 Advances in RTR — mesh clustering, GPU culling, indirect/multi-draw and few CPU-issued draw calls in Assassin's Creed Unity and the RedLynx pipeline — [Advances in RTR 2015](https://advances.realtimerendering.com/s2015/index.html)
- GPU picking: draw every object's ID as a flat colour into an offscreen target with depth testing and read the pixel under the cursor; an 8-bit RGBA target encodes about 4 billion IDs (0 = nothing); rendering through a 1×1-pixel sub-frustum avoids drawing the whole screen — [WebGL2 Fundamentals: Picking](https://webgl2fundamentals.org/webgl/lessons/webgl-picking.html)
- Scale reference from a shipping web jewellery renderer: the vendor claims several hundred diamonds can be rendered at once, including on mobile — [iJewel3D docs](https://docs.ijewel3d.com/introduction/ijewel.html); drei flags extra bounces and dispersion as expensive — [drei docs](https://drei.docs.pmnd.rs/shaders/mesh-refraction-material)
- Stone sizes for the projected-size arithmetic below: 8ss ≈ 2.3 mm, 10ss ≈ 2.8 mm — [Wikipedia: Rhinestone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhinestone); available range ss2–ss48 — [Preciosa benchmark PDF](https://img.preciosacomponents.com/files/landingpage/lp-4/downloads/Preciosa_Chaton_Rose_MAXIMA_Benchmark.pdf)

### Inferences
- **How many elements really exist.** With an assumed 0.6–0.9 m² of garment (unsourced assumption), hexagonal full coverage holds on the order of 10^5 stones at 10ss (2.8 mm) and a few 10^4 at the larger sizes typical of feature areas; sequins at 4 mm pitch are similar. A million discrete elements only arises for glitter flakes (sub-millimetre, tens of millions per m²), which must be statistical (section 1). Design the instance path for 10^3–2×10^5 and treat 10^6 as a stress test.
- **Projected size drives everything.** If a 1.5 m gymnast fills about 85% of the viewport height, a pixel is about 1.6 mm at 1080p and 0.8 mm at 2160p: an 8–10ss stone covers roughly 1.4–1.7 px (1080p) or 2.8–3.4 px (4K), larger stones a few pixels more; individual facets are always sub-pixel at full-body distance. In an extreme close-up (5 cm across the screen) a stone is 100–200 px wide and only a few hundred are in view.
- **Proposed LOD chain (selected per instance by projected diameter d in pixels, with hysteresis and a cross-fade band):**
  - LOD0, d ≳ 32 px: full facet mesh (table + crown facets + girdle bevel, about 40–80 triangles) with in-shader analytic tracing, dispersion and thin-film; MSAA on silhouettes.
  - LOD1, 6–32 px: reduced mesh (about 16–24 triangles); per-facet shading from the precomputed exit-lobe table (no tracing).
  - LOD2, 1–6 px: one quad (or point sprite) per stone oriented to the fabric; the fragment shader evaluates the stone's lobes analytically and outputs an anti-aliased disc; when d < about 1.5 px the splat is clamped to that size and its radiance scaled by the area ratio so energy is conserved.
  - LOD3, d < 1 px or beyond an instance budget: no geometry; stones are folded into the fabric shader as a glint lobe driven by a stone-density/coverage texture in pattern space, using the same per-stone seeds.
  - The invariant that makes transitions seamless: every LOD draws from the same per-stone discrete lobe set and seed, so the same stone flashes at the same light/view configuration with the same integrated energy; only the spatial resolution of the flash changes.
- **Triangle budget.** Full-body view: everything is LOD2/3, so 10^5 stones are 2×10^5 triangles. Worst mid-range case (10^5 stones all at LOD1): about 2×10^6 triangles — fine on discrete GPUs, heavy for integrated ones, hence a per-frame instance budget that pushes the smallest stones to LOD2. Close-up: a few hundred LOD0 stones, fill-rate bound. These are estimates to be validated on target hardware.
- **Per-instance layout (proposed, 32 bytes, GPU-friendly, edit-in-place):** surface anchor (triangle or pattern-piece id + barycentric/UV, so stones follow drape and can be re-projected) or a baked float3 position; octahedral-encoded normal + in-plane rotation (3×16-bit); size class and cut id (2×8-bit, indexing mesh/LOD and lobe table); colour/coating id (16-bit, indexing a palette row with tint, index, dispersion, film thickness); random seed (8–16-bit); flags (selected, hovered, hidden, locked). At 32 B, 10^5 stones are 3.2 MB and 10^6 are 32 MB; even 64-byte matrices for 10^6 fit the 128 MB default storage binding. In WebGL2 the same records go in instanced vertex attributes or an RGBA32F/integer data texture indexed by `gl_InstanceID` (a 4096² texture holds 16.7 M texels).
- **Culling.** Back-face culling per stone (stone normal vs view) removes about half; frustum culling per cluster (stones bucketed by pattern piece/spatial cell, 256–1024 per cluster) is cheap on the CPU; in WebGPU a compute pass can do per-instance frustum + normal-cone + depth-pyramid occlusion tests and compact into the visible list feeding one indirect draw per LOD (four indirect draws in one buffer). In WebGL2 (no compute) use CPU cluster culling and let the vertex shader collapse back-facing/out-of-LOD instances to degenerate triangles; the body occludes via the normal depth test.
- **Painting/editing.** Keep a CPU-side array as the source of truth, append new instances at the end (swap-remove on delete) and upload only the dirty byte range each frame (`writeBuffer` with offset in WebGPU; `bufferSubData` in WebGL2). A brush stroke adding tens of stones per frame is a few hundred bytes. Grow buffers geometrically (allocate 2× when full) to avoid per-stroke reallocation. Undo/redo is a log of record edits.
- **Picking.** Render stone IDs (index + 1) into an R32Uint (WebGPU) or RGBA8 (WebGL2) target using the LOD2 quad for all stones so that picking is cheap and tolerant (give the pick splat a minimum radius of a few pixels so sub-pixel stones remain selectable), read back asynchronously one frame late; for single clicks use the 1×1-frustum trick, for brush/lasso selection keep a low-resolution full ID buffer and scan the region on the CPU, or resolve the selection in a compute pass against the instance buffer directly.
- **Memory.** Instances (≤ 32–64 MB worst case), a handful of stone meshes (kilobytes), lobe tables (about 0.5 MB per stone type), glint noise/LTC textures (well under 1 MB), ID buffer (4 bytes per pixel) — negligible next to HDR render targets and environment maps.

### Gaps
- No verified public benchmark of instance counts/triangle throughput at 60 fps on integrated GPUs in WebGL2 or WebGPU; the budgets above are estimates.
- Octahedral impostors (Ryan Brucks' technique) could not be re-verified (the article page returned no content) and are not cited; for flat-back stones a view-dependent impostor atlas is probably unnecessary given the analytic quad approach.
- Not verified here: WebGPU's MSAA sample-count restrictions, three.js partial-update API names, three.js/Babylon GPU-picking helpers, and native-API specifics (mesh shaders, Vulkan/D3D12 multi-draw-indirect-count).
- How stones should follow simulated cloth (re-anchoring cost under re-simulation) depends on the engine/cloth design covered by other researchers.

## 6. Temporal stability and anti-aliasing of sparkle; HDR pipeline, bloom, glare/star streaks, exposure; how cameras and eyes render sparkle and what "accuracy" means

### Takeaway
Sub-pixel highlights must be filtered at the source — widen the lobe to the pixel/time footprint and keep the energy — because image-space fixes (TAA history clamping, luminance-weighted resolves, bloom firefly suppression) either flicker or throw away exactly the energy that makes sparkle. The perceived "star" of a rhinestone is mostly created by the imaging system (eye or camera point-spread function, aperture diffraction, sensor clipping), so an accurate renderer delivers physically scaled HDR sparkle energy and then applies an explicit, selectable glare model.

### Cited Findings
- Kaplanyan, Hill, Patney, Lefohn, "Filtering Distributions of Normals for Shading Antialiasing", HPG 2016 (best paper): glossy highlights on curved surfaces are small relative to a pixel yet carry high energy, so they alias; the method estimates a filtering region in the half-vector (slope) domain from the pixel footprint and filters the NDF over it; GPU-friendly, temporally stable, compatible with deferred shading and normal maps — [NVIDIA Research](https://research.nvidia.com/publication/2016-06_filtering-distributions-normals-shading-antialiasing)
- Tokuyoshi & Kaplanyan, "Improved Geometric Specular Antialiasing", I3D 2019, pp. 8:1–8:8 (paper, slides, video available) — [Square Enix publications](https://www.jp.square-enix.com/tech/publications.html)
- Glint-specific anti-aliasing: Chermain et al. 2021 (geometric glint AA with LEAN-compatible slope filtering) removes most inconsistent appearing/disappearing of glints on curved animated surfaces — [paper PDF](https://publis.icube.unistra.fr/docs/15022/Chermain2021RealTime.pdf); Deliot & Belcour obtain stability by interpolating random seeds on spatial and angular grids (glint "lifespan") and by blending LODs at the level of binomial parameters; their temporal claims are qualitative (video) — [ar5iv HTML](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2306.05051); Kneiphof & Klein 2025 note that glint angular lifetime is not modelled in their IBL method and flag principled control of it as future work — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.02674v1)
- Temporal antialiasing: Karis, "High-Quality Temporal Supersampling" (SIGGRAPH 2014 Advances course) describes UE4's TAA and its fixes for HDR, ghosting and flickering — [Advances in RTR 2014](https://advances.realtimerendering.com/s2014/index.html); Yang, Liu, Salvi, "A Survey of Temporal Antialiasing Techniques", CGF 39(2):607–621, 2020, structures TAA into sample accumulation and history validation and analyses its open image-quality problems — [DOI 10.1111/cgf.14018](https://doi.org/10.1111/cgf.14018)
- Luminance-weighted filtering: Karis's tone-mapping note proposes weighting each sample by 1/(1 + luma) (equivalent to tone map → average → inverse tone map) to stop a single very bright sample dominating a filtered result — [Graphic Rants: Tone mapping](http://graphicrants.blogspot.com/2013/12/tone-mapping.html)
- Bloom: Jimenez, "Next Generation Post Processing in Call of Duty: Advanced Warfare" (SIGGRAPH 2014 Advances course): a pyramidal down/up-sampling filter chain designed for temporal stability, with no bright-pass threshold under PBR and a low blend factor (about 0.04) — [Jimenez's page](https://www.iryoku.com/next-generation-post-processing-in-call-of-duty-advanced-warfare); a public re-implementation documents the 13-tap downsample and 3×3 tent upsample kernels, the energy-preserving weights, the final `mix(hdr, bloom, ~0.04)`, an R11G11B10F mip chain, and that the "Karis average" firefly fix on the first downsample makes the bloom slightly weaker — [LearnOpenGL: Physically Based Bloom](https://learnopengl.com/Guest-Articles/2022/Phys.-Based-Bloom)
- Unreal Engine offers standard (sum of Gaussians, fast, not energy-conserving) and convolution bloom (FFT convolution with a kernel image that represents how a camera or eye scatters a point source; energy-conserving, costlier, recommended for cinematics/high-end hardware); the documentation notes that streaks and lens flares are not covered by classic bloom — [Epic: Bloom (UE 5.8)](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/en-us/unreal-engine/bloom-in-unreal-engine)
- Glare in the human eye and cameras: Spencer, Shirley, Zimmerman, Greenberg, "Physically-based glare effects for digital images", SIGGRAPH 1995, pp. 325–334 — [DOI 10.1145/218380.218466](https://doi.org/10.1145/218380.218466); Ritschel, Ihrke, Frisvad, Coppens, Myszkowski, Seidel, "Temporal Glare: Real-Time Dynamic Simulation of the Scattering in the Human Eye", CGF 28(2):183–192, 2009 — an eye-anatomy-based, GPU real-time, time-varying glare model; psychophysics showed glare raises perceived brightness and dynamic glare is often preferred — [DOI 10.1111/j.1467-8659.2009.01357.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-8659.2009.01357.x); Hullin, Eisemann, Seidel, Lee, "Physically-based real-time lens flare rendering", ACM TOG 30(4), 2011 — [DOI 10.1145/2010324.1965003](https://doi.org/10.1145/2010324.1965003)
- Star-shaped spikes: camera iris blades form a polygon when stopped down and each edge produces a pair of opposite spikes — an even blade count gives as many spikes as blades, an odd count twice as many; in human vision, eyelashes, eyelid edges and lens suture lines produce spikes that shimmer and shift; cross-screen "star" filters use fine gratings or prisms to create star patterns deliberately — [Wikipedia: Diffraction spike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffraction_spike)
- HDR in browsers: WebGPU canvases can be configured with `rgba16float` and `toneMapping: { mode: "extended" }` to display values brighter than SDR white on HDR screens (Chrome 129) — [Chrome: What's new in WebGPU 129](https://developer.chrome.com/blog/new-in-webgpu-129); compact HDR render targets (`rg11b10ufloat` renderable/blendable/multisampled) and filterable/blendable 32-bit float textures are optional WebGPU features — [MDN: GPUSupportedFeatures](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/GPUSupportedFeatures)
- Gem shaders and MSAA interact badly if the in-shader tracer depends on interpolated normals: the three-mesh-bvh diamond example disables hardware antialiasing for this reason — [diamond.js source](https://github.com/gkjohnson/three-mesh-bvh/blob/master/example/diamond.js)

### Inferences
- **Filter where the signal is generated.** For glitter, the counting models already integrate over the pixel footprint. For stones and sequins rendered as instances, give every specular lobe a minimum angular width equal to the larger of (a) the light's angular radius, (b) the variation of the half-vector across the stone's pixel footprint (Kaplanyan-style), and (c) the half-vector change per frame at the current camera/light angular velocity, and renormalise the lobe so its integral is unchanged. (c) is a temporal analogue of specular AA: it guarantees each flash lasts a few frames instead of one, trading peak intensity for duration at constant energy. This is my proposal, extrapolated from the cited NDF-filtering work.
- **Do not let TAA arbitrate sparkle.** Neighbourhood clamping treats an isolated HDR pixel as an outlier: it either rejects history (flicker) or clamps the sparkle (dimming). Recommended: render sparkle (stone/sequin specular and glint lobes) into a separate HDR "sparkle" target that is excluded from history clamping; anti-alias geometry with MSAA 4× (stone splats use analytic coverage instead of relying on sample count); when camera, lights and garment are idle, switch to progressive jittered accumulation with no clamping (converges in 16–64 frames to a reference-quality still, which is what a design tool is judged on). If TAA/TSR is used for the rest of the frame, composite the sparkle layer after it.
- **Energy-preserving clamps.** Use footprint clamps (minimum splat size with radiance scaled by area; minimum lobe width with renormalisation) rather than radiance clamps. Luminance-weighted averaging (Karis) and bloom firefly suppression are biased toward dark and should be confined to the first bloom downsample, with awareness that they reduce sparkle bloom (as the cited bloom article observes); never apply them to the main resolve of the sparkle layer.
- **HDR pipeline.** Linear scene-referred rendering in 16-bit float (or R11G11B10F where alpha is not needed); sparkle peaks will be 10^2–10^4 times diffuse white with small lights, so 8-bit or clamped intermediates destroy the effect. Bloom without threshold (Jimenez chain) gives the soft veil; add a dedicated glare pass for the star: convolve the sparkle layer (which is sparse) with a point-spread kernel — either a small streak filter in 2–3 directions or an FFT/kernel-image convolution as in Unreal — restricted to the sparkle layer so cost stays low. Exposure should be set from the garment's diffuse level (sparkles are meant to clip); auto-exposure must ignore the sparkle layer or it will pump.
- **What "accurate" should mean.** Two different observers produce different sparkle from the same radiance: the eye adds a ciliary corona/needle pattern that fluctuates in time and a small bloom (Spencer; Ritschel), while a camera adds aperture-dependent diffraction spikes (count set by blade number), sensor/lens bloom, clipping to white with a coloured rim, and optionally a star filter. Photos and broadcast footage — the references designers actually compare against — are camera renditions. So the renderer should be radiometrically correct up to the HDR buffer and then offer named "observer" presets (naked eye in arena, broadcast camera, phone photo, studio photo with star filter), each a documented glare kernel plus exposure/tone curve. On HDR displays the sparkle can be shown brighter than paper white, which reduces the need for exaggerated glare.
- **Dispersion colour and bloom.** Because fire colours are saturated and far above white, bloom/glare must be applied before tone mapping and per channel, otherwise coloured flashes desaturate to white halos.

### Gaps
- Kawase's light-streak filter (GDC 2003) and other classic streak/"anamorphic" techniques could not be re-verified and are not cited.
- No source quantifies real rhinestone sparkle radiance relative to diffuse white under arena lighting, nor measured eye/camera PSFs specific to this use; the dynamic-range figures above are reasoning from specular reflection of small lights.
- The full texts of the TAA survey and of the specular-AA papers were not re-read for their exact statements about sub-pixel highlight flicker; the TAA interaction described under Inferences is standard practice knowledge rather than a quoted finding.
- No evidence was found on how broadcast gymnastics coverage is actually shot (star filters, shutter/iris settings).

## 7. Lighting dependence: small intense sources, HDRIs vs explicit lights, environment importance sampling for glints, and interaction of lighting edits with precomputation

### Takeaway
Sparkle is a function of light size as much as of the material: every verified glint method started with point lights, and only in 2024–2025 were area lights and environment maps handled properly (with residual limits on colour and occlusion). For an interactive tool the robust recipe is explicit small area lights (with true angular size) for the sparkle term, an HDRI for the smooth term and gem refraction, and precomputation that depends only on stone geometry — never on lighting.

### Cited Findings
- Deliot & Belcour 2023 supports only punctual lights, with cost linear in the number of lights because counts cannot be shared between lights — [ar5iv HTML](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2306.05051)
- Kneiphof & Klein 2024: area lights via LTC with a locally constant NDF approximation for small lights; the light's opening angle becomes a physical parameter; earlier implementations evaluated glints only for the mean light direction; light visibility is not accounted for and textured lights are not handled — [arXiv HTML v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.13611v2)
- Kneiphof & Klein 2025: environment lighting by splitting the map into K = 4 or 8 radiance levels (log-spaced between the map's min and max, dimmest level zeroed for below-horizon), prefiltered with the NDF; K = 4 shows visible differences for very high dynamic range maps; prefiltering can run every frame so lights/environment stay fully dynamic; no occlusion; nearby coloured sources yield white glints — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.02674v1)
- Kemppinen et al. 2025 supports importance sampling of the glinty NDF and shows image-based lighting — [project page](https://perso.telecom-paristech.fr/boubek/papers/Glinty/)
- Offline reference for glints under complex lighting: Gamboa et al., "Scalable appearance filtering for complex lighting effects", ACM TOG 37(6), 2018 — [reference list](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.02674v1)
- Extracting discrete lights from an HDRI: Debevec, "A median cut algorithm for light probe sampling", SIGGRAPH 2005 Posters — [DOI 10.1145/1186954.1187029](https://doi.org/10.1145/1186954.1187029)
- Gem appearance is environment-driven in practice: iJewel3D's documentation says sparkle and fire depend heavily on the assigned HDRI and exposes several environment-rotation controls to tune them — [iJewel3D docs: Diamond Materials](https://docs.ijewel3d.com/playground/diamond-materials.html); the gemological literature likewise ties fire to lighting and viewing conditions — [Wikipedia: Dispersion (optics)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dispersion_(optics))
- Engine precedents for the indirect-light problem: Unity's AxF car-paint shader uses a fixed half-vector angle for flakes under reflection probes and ray-traced indirect light because the true angle is unknown — [Unity HDRP AxF manual](https://docs.unity3d.com/Packages/com.unity.render-pipelines.high-definition@14.0/manual/AxF-Shader.html); Unreal's glints are unavailable in the Blendable GBuffer format — [Epic: Overview of Substrate Materials](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/en-us/unreal-engine/overview-of-substrate-materials-in-unreal-engine)

### Inferences
- **Light rig for sparkle.** Model competition/show lighting as a set of small, intense sphere/disc area lights with physically meaningful angular size (a luminaire a metre across at 20 m subtends about 3°; a bare LED far less). The angular size sets how often and how long each facet fires, and whether dispersion fire is visible; expose it directly as a "light size" control because it is the main sparkle dial for the user.
- **Hybrid HDRI strategy.** Keep an HDRI for ambient/smooth reflections and gem refraction, but extract its brightest emitters (median cut or thresholded connected components) into explicit area lights at load time and remove them from the map used for the smooth lobe to avoid double counting. This gives coloured, occludable, shadow-casting sparkle sources (which the 2025 IBL-glint method cannot) while keeping its radiance-level prefilter as an optional path for the residual environment. A bounded light count (for example the 8–16 strongest) keeps the per-light glint cost predictable.
- **Precomputation vs interactive lighting.** Keep all precomputation lighting-independent: per-cut exit-lobe tables (function of geometry, index, coating), glint noise/dictionary textures and LTC tables never change with lighting. Lighting-dependent work is limited to environment prefiltering (already a per-frame-capable operation in engines and in the cited method) and light extraction, both of which can be amortised over a few frames after a lighting edit while the old result is cross-faded.
- **Occlusion.** None of the verified glint methods handles light visibility; shadowing must come from the engine's shadow maps applied per light to the sparkle term (another reason to prefer explicit lights over IBL for sparkle). Stones on the shadowed side of the body must not sparkle.
- **Per-stone environment lookups** (for LOD1/LOD2 lobes) should use the prefiltered mip matching each lobe's solid angle; with extracted lights removed from the map, these lookups contribute the soft "brilliance" and the explicit lights contribute the hard "scintillation".

### Gaps
- No verified data on real arena/broadcast lighting geometry (luminaire sizes, counts, distances) was collected here; another researcher covers the lighting pipeline.
- Whether Unreal's Substrate glints respond to sky/IBL or only analytic lights is unverified.
- No published real-time method was found for dispersion-correct environment sampling of gems beyond per-channel lookups; importance sampling of bright emitters through a gem (which light reaches the eye via which facet) is handled in my proposal by the precomputed lobe table, which is untested.

## 8. Validation: measured data and metrics that could make "accurate" quantifiable

### Takeaway
Sparkle already has industrial metrology — multi-angle instruments with built-in cameras report sparkle and coarseness grades at defined illumination angles for effect coatings — and those definitions (directional-light "glint impression" versus diffuse-light "coarseness") transfer directly to glitter fabric and stoned areas. Public BTF databases are not a usable ground truth for sparkle (too coarse angularly, no glitter/sequin samples identified), so validation will need purpose-shot reference photography plus offline renders.

### Cited Findings
- Kirchner, van den Kieboom, Njo, Supèr, Gottenbos, "Observation of visual texture of metallic and pearlescent materials", Color Research & Application 32(4):256–266, 2007: defines two texture parameters for effect coatings — Diffuse Coarseness (under diffuse light) and Glint Impression (under directional light) — with standardised observation procedures and good observer repeatability, and concludes the two illumination conditions must be characterised separately — [DOI 10.1002/col.20328](https://doi.org/10.1002/col.20328)
- X-Rite MA-T12 handheld multi-angle spectrophotometer: 12 spectral measurement angles (6 illuminations × 2 pick-ups) plus a colour-calibrated RGB camera at 40 px/mm; reports Sparkle Grade, a Colour Sparkle parameter and Diffuse Coarseness at several illumination geometries (15°, 30°, 45°, 80° aspecular variants and diffuse); cites ASTM E2194, E308, E1164, D2244, DIN 6175 and related standards; intended for effect finishes with interference/metallic pigments — [X-Rite MA-T12](https://www.xrite.com/categories/portable-spectrophotometers/ma-t-family/ma-t12)
- Measured flake appearance is used in production real-time rendering: Unity HDRP's AxF car-paint material renders measured angular flake slices (a BTF) from X-Rite AxF files — [Unity HDRP AxF manual](https://docs.unity3d.com/Packages/com.unity.render-pipelines.high-definition@14.0/manual/AxF-Shader.html); Unreal 5.8 adds AxF import into Substrate — [Epic: UE 5.8 release notes](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/en-us/unreal-engine/unreal-engine-5-8-release-notes)
- Bonn BTF database: the UBO2014 sets were captured with a camera dome of 151 view × 151 light directions (22,801 images per material, cameras spaced about 15° apart on the hemisphere) for 5 cm × 5 cm samples at 512×512 texels (central 400×400 ≈ 3.9 cm provided); earlier UBO2003/ATRIUM sets use 81×81 directions. The page's sample descriptions contain no glitter, sparkle, sequin or flake materials (closest: a colour checker with two metallic paints and a fabric with a glossy silver yarn) — [Univ. Bonn: BTFDBB](https://cg.cs.uni-bonn.de/data/btfdbb-btf-database-bonn-and-measurement-lab)
- Research practice for validating real-time glints is comparison against offline ground truth: Kneiphof & Klein 2025 render references in Mitsuba 3; Zhu et al.'s survey benchmarks methods on a unified platform for visual effect, speed and memory — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.02674v1); [Springer](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s41095-022-0280-x)
- Manufacturer-side optical claims exist but are not public measurements: Preciosa refers to third-party optical testing of light return versus its previous cut and competitors and gives a count of unique reflected rays, without publishing method or data — [Preciosa MAXIMA FAQ PDF](https://img.preciosacomponents.com/files/landingpage/lp-4/downloads/Preciosa_Chaton_Rose_MAXIMA_Frequently_Asked_Questions_EN.pdf)

### Inferences
- **A practical accuracy protocol (proposed).** (1) Build a small physical reference set: swatches of the target fabrics with glitter, sequins and stone grids (several sizes, crystal and AB). (2) Photograph each in linear raw with a calibrated camera under a single small light at a few standard geometries (e.g. light 15°, 45°, 75° from the normal, camera on the normal — mirroring effect-coating practice) and under diffuse light, plus a slow rotation sequence. (3) Reproduce the set-up virtually (same light size, distance, lens, aperture/PSF, exposure). (4) Compare statistics rather than pixels: number of sparkle spots per cm² above a contrast threshold, sparkle intensity histogram, sparkle area/size distribution after the PSF, colour distribution of sparkles (for AB/holographic), mean and angular falloff of the area-averaged reflectance (checks the far-field NDF), and for the rotation sequence the sparkle lifetime (temporal autocorrelation). Pass criteria can be stated as tolerances on these metrics.
- These metrics map onto the industrial quantities: "sparkle grade/glint impression" ↔ spot count × intensity under directional light; "diffuse coarseness/graininess" ↔ low-contrast texture under diffuse light; "colour sparkle" ↔ chromatic spread of spots. If budget allows, an effect-coating instrument reading of the glitter fabrics would give vendor-neutral numbers to match.
- **Internal consistency tests** that need no physical data: (a) LOD invariance — area-integrated radiance and sparkle counts of a stoned patch must agree across LOD0–LOD3 within tolerance for a sweep of distances; (b) far-field convergence — the glint lobe must match the smooth GGX/Beckmann lobe when flake count per pixel is large; (c) energy check per stone against an offline path-traced render of the same cut (white-furnace-style, noting that the Deliot–Belcour lobe is by its authors' admission not energy-conserving); (d) temporal — no single-frame flashes above a threshold during a standard orbit.
- BTF data are angularly far too sparse (about 15° spacing) to capture sparkle, whose features are a degree or less wide; they are useful only for validating the smooth fabric base, not the sparkle layer.

### Gaps
- Could not verify details I recall about BYK's multi-angle instrument (sparkle intensity, sparkle area and graininess under 15°/45°/75° illumination), about CIE/EMPIR work on standard sparkle and graininess measurement scales (e.g. Ferrero et al.), or any ASTM standard specific to sparkle; the BYK and EMPIR pages could not be reached.
- The UTIA BTF database could not be reached; no dataset containing sequin or glitter-fabric samples was identified anywhere.
- No published perceptual thresholds for sparkle differences (just-noticeable differences) relevant to fashion visualisation were found.

## 9. Example implementations (public shaders, repositories, engine features) and how fashion-CAD tools and Substance render rhinestones and sequins today

### Takeaway
Reusable public code exists for every glitter method and for web gem refraction, but nothing public combines them into "thousands of editable stones on a garment". Mainstream fashion CAD treats glitter and gems as offline-render-only materials or 2D artwork effects and has no documented rhinestone/sequin placement or real-time sparkle — a clear gap this product can own.

### Inferences
- **Recommended build-or-borrow map.** Glitter: port `Glints2023/2024.hlsl` (Deliot–Belcour + area lights) or the Kemppinen Shadertoy; licence terms of the Google-Drive-hosted Deliot–Belcour code and of the Bonn repositories are unstated, so treat them as references and re-implement from the papers (Chermain's code is MIT; three-mesh-bvh, N8Programs' diamond shader and drei are MIT). Stones close-up: write the analytic convex tracer (simpler than the BVH route) using the three-mesh-bvh/drei shader as a behavioural reference for bounce loop, fast-chroma dispersion and instancing. AB/iridescence: reuse the glTF/three.js Belcour–Barla implementation. Everything else (lobe tables, LOD chain, sparkle layer, picking) is bespoke.
- **Competitive position.** The three mainstream fashion-CAD help centers document glitter/gems as render-only or as 2D print effects, and none documents stone-by-stone placement with real-time sparkle. Rhinestone layout today lives in separate 2D template tools (unverified recollection) and offline renders. A tool that shows editable, physically plausible sparkle at 60 fps from full-body to macro would be differentiated on exactly the axis the brief emphasises.
- Substance-style procedural glitter in fashion CAD ("Glitter Print" with flake size and randomness, grid at zero randomness) is texture-space and static — it bakes flakes into maps and so cannot twinkle correctly with view/light changes the way a glint BRDF does; this is a concrete, demonstrable quality difference.

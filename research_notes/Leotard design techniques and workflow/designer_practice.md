## Who designs leotards at each brand, and what have they said about how they work?

### Takeaway
Named design staff can now be listed for GK Elite, OZONE, Sylvia P, Quatro and Snowflake, which closes most of the project's known gap; Alpha Factor and Moreau Sport yield only managers, and no designer was found at Destira, Plum, Dreamlight, Pastorelli or Mondor. Many team designs are originated by coaches and athletes, with the brand's designer and sales rep translating the idea into something wearable, affordable and producible.

### Inferences
- The recurring profile is an insider with apparel or graphic training: ex-gymnast or gym-parent founders, and apparel or graphic graduates hired young who stay well over a decade (Diaz, Brown). Gymnastics knowledge seems to matter as much as drawing skill.
- The professional designer persona describes one author. The evidence suggests a three-way loop of originator (coach, athlete, federation), translator (designer) and go-between (rep), and the originator often owns the creative direction. The app may serve that loop, not only the designer.
- At some brands (Snowflake, OZONE's Brown) design and sales are one person, so design time competes with selling.

## What is the starting artefact?

### Takeaway
Designers rarely start blank. The documented starting points are a client's reference image, a client's drawing, a catalogue style, or a mock-up in team colours; for collections, a survey and mood boards. GK says a new design itself begins as a line drawing. No public example of the professional template was found.

## In what order do decisions get made?

### Takeaway
No source states "silhouette, then lines, then fabric, then crystals" as a fixed order, and the evidence does not support it as written. Constraints and references come first; colour identity is fixed early for teams; function precedes decoration; fabric swatches arrive after the mock-up; stones stay adjustable to the end. The back is treated as its own decision.

## How is construction expressed, and what is left to the pattern maker?

### Takeaway
Designers do write down construction: GK's designers produce paperwork passing sewing and embellishment information to pattern makers and the factory floor. But the designer's drawing is not the pattern. A separate pattern department engineers fit per fabric, and print and embellishment artwork are separate production files. No real leotard design sheet or tech pack is public.

### Inferences
- Likely split of decisions. The designer owns look, line placement, print art, crystal motifs, fabric choice and functional features, and writes the instructions. The pattern department owns fit, seam allowances, stretch adjustment and grading of the garment. The print or production designer owns print-ready and embellishment files. The sewing floor owns finishing and reports problems back.
- Because front and back are decorated flat before closing, designers probably think of each as a flat panel, which may be why flats persist. A body-first tool should show those panels and warn when art crosses a closing seam.
- Job splits suggest the app's three edit layers should be garment pieces, print art and embellishment, matching who touches what at a factory.

## How does iteration work, and what kills or changes a design?

### Takeaway
Iteration is client-led and repeats at two stages, drawing and sample. Sources speak of several rounds and months, never a number. The most concrete failure is that a design can match its drawing and still need changing once seen on a body. In the NCAA account, cost and feasibility are checked as a separate step, after a design has been sketched.

### Cited Findings

### Inferences

## How do they think visually?

### Takeaway
Stated concerns are symmetry versus busyness, lines that flatter and direct the eye without chopping the body, a back strong enough to change the whole look, tonal contrast of texture, and crystals that build pattern at close range. Tastes shift quickly, so specific trends date within a few years.

### Cited Findings
- Busy versus clean: McKeown said the US team tended to symmetrical, regal designs; some countries prefer busier looks, while others think designs that chop up the body distract from the routine. — [BuzzFeed 2012](https://www.buzzfeed.com/amyodell/everything-you-ever-wanted-to-know-about-team-usa) [read] [dated 2012]
- Lines and the eye: the Chiles zig-zag cut, picked out in crystals, is described as accentuating body lines and drawing the eye across the patterns (article narration, not a direct designer quote). — [NBC LA 2024](https://www.nbclosangeles.com/paris-2024-summer-olympics/jordan-chiles-2024-paris-olympics-gymnastics-beyonce/) [read]. Douglass wanted the flag used literally while still highlighting the gymnasts' bodies. — [NBC Philadelphia 2016](https://nbcphiladelphia.com/news/local/reading-company-bedazzles-at-olympics/91046/?amp=1) [read]

## How long does a design take, and where is the friction?

### Takeaway
The few documented custom designs took about five to six months end to end; Olympic collections take 18 months to two years; one maker reports cutting a single drawing from three weeks to a day. Friction, as far as public sources show it, is mostly translation and visibility: ideas passed through reps and sketches, proofs that do not show the body, cost seen late, and colour that differs between proof and delivery.

### Inferences
Friction points and how the app could ease them, all inference:
- **Translation loss** (coach or athlete idea, rep, designer, proof). Let originator and designer work on the same 3D canvas, including a live session mode.
- **Blank-page cost.** Open from a base in team colours, a pinned reference image, or a sketch, because those are the observed starts.
- **Flat-to-body surprise.** Pose and turn the mannequin early, and show where lines land, including the leg line and bent arms.
- **Back changes as rework.** Make the back a swappable part that leaves front art and stones untouched.
- **Cost seen late.** Keep a running stone and piece count with an indicative price, and offer a lighter stone version of the same design.
- **Colour mistrust.** Name colours against a reference (Pantone or the maker's swatch) and print that on the proof.
- **Grading art per size.** Automate placement and stone grading, with per-size counts, since it is hand work today.
- **Hand-off rework.** Export a client proof and separate production items: pieces, print-ready art, embellishment template, sewing and embellishment notes, and vector files that open in Illustrator.
- **Option comparison.** Support several versions side by side and a set view, since colleges buy three or four differing leotards and collections are chosen from many.
- **Learning construction.** Brink's factory tour and the owner's overlay discovery suggest a tool that shows how a line gets made teaches clients and yields better designs.

## What vocabulary do they use?

### Takeaway
The trade's words separate drawing stages (sketch, line drawing, proof, mockup), garment structure (pattern pieces, back patterns), decoration (sublimation art, jewels, diamante, appliqué) and order types (stock, custom). The app's labels should follow these words.

### Inferences
- Reviewers and coaches talk about a leotard as `body`, `sleeves`, `V`, `straps` and `back`, so region names on the mannequin should use those words, not "panel 3".
- `Proof` is the word for the approval image. `Sample` is the physical one. The app should keep these distinct.

# Takeaway

**The same picture can come from different cut pieces.** A mesh upper bodice with two opaque plates has at least four
documented builds, and catalogue copy rarely says which was used, so a design tool has to store the build as a separate
choice from the look.[^notes] No brand publishes stitch-level construction: the how-to evidence comes from Jalie's home
patterns and Marie Porter's costume write-ups standing in for factory practice.[^report]

# The four builds

| Build | What it is | What is cut | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Seam enclosed by lining | Mesh is sewn to the body along one line and the lining is sewn over the same seam, enclosing its raw edges | Body and mesh meet at a seam; the lining stops at it | One pattern maker[^jade]; factory use unknown |
| Window from behind (reverse appliqué, illusion inset) | Mesh lies behind the body fabric and is stitched in place; the body is cut away inside the stitching, or cut first with about 1 in of overlap | The body has a hole; the mesh is a backing piece larger than the hole | Fact: several sources[^tessa][^porter-neck] |
| Plate on a whole base | Mesh and lining are cut whole; the plate is stitched on round its edge | The base stays one piece at cutting; the plate is an extra piece with no allowance | One maker, in skating and costume work[^flames] |
| Plate under the mesh | A shaped opaque plate lies beneath a mesh layer, giving shine through a haze | The mesh stays whole; the plate sits beneath it | Look is fact[^lunette][^cobalt]; no how-to found; how the plate is held is unknown |

A fifth, a true double layer with two separately finished necklines, rests on one listing and a photo.[^report]

# What happens under a plate

"The fabric under a plate stays whole" holds for what is cut, not always for what is finished. Jalie trims the base
about 1 cm from the stitching to reduce bulk[^kate]; Porter lifts and trims each layer as she stacks colours, or cuts
pieces in advance so overlaps are a small planned lip, with darker fabric on top so it does not show through.[^porter-applique][^flames]
**[INFERRED]** Every plate needs an "under" setting: left whole, trimmed to a margin, cut away, or lip only.

# Choosing a build **[INFERRED]**

Two reasons are stated by makers: simple lines are seamed and swirly ones overlaid,[^chalkbucket] and fabric rules some
builds out.[^report] The rest of this table is our inference from the sources; no maker has confirmed it. It may be
offered as a suggestion, never as a rule.

| If the line or plate... | It points to |
|---|---|
| has mesh touching it along one top edge only | a seam enclosed by lining |
| has mesh on two or more sides, or mesh between plates | a plate on a whole base or under the mesh (piecing would need L-shaped openings) |
| has tight curves, sharp inside corners or points | a plate on top |
| should read as shine through a haze | under the mesh |
| must read as a raised or textured line | an applied strip or stones, not print |
| crosses a side, shoulder or crotch seam | print, or a plate finished by hand across the seam |

# Reading a photo **[INFERRED]**

Mesh haze over a shiny area means the plate is under the mesh; a narrow stitched edge with no haze means it is on top; a
soft ridge with no haze means a pieced seam. This will not settle every garment: a photo cannot separate a backed window
from a pieced inset.[^report]

How designers actually decide is [an open question](/product/questions/how-designers-choose-a-build.md).

# Our solver models fit, not fabric behaviour

**Our fit solver is a sound geometric fit, not a realistic cloth simulation, and it should not be sped up yet.** Its method belongs to a published family, and it gives a credible overall shape wherever fabric is pulled tight in at least one direction; our own estimate for those areas is several millimetres to a centimetre, which nothing has yet confirmed against a real garment. It is not reliable for what a viewer looks at most: the leg line (set by an elastic strength we guessed), the seat, crotch and small of the back (set by a surface computed before the fit), spare fabric (shown as a squeezed print, never as a wrinkle), and every pose other than the one it was solved in. Public simulators include more physics than ours: friction, bending, true contact and, in some, measured fabric. They are not better proven for this job, though. **No public project we audited checks a stretch fit against reality**, and the research that comes closest mostly measures pressure at a few points on limbs and bras ([Frontiers 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/bioengineering-and-biotechnology/articles/10.3389/fbioe.2024.1418047/full)), with shape checked on one leggings wearer at best ([Ye 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/bioengineering-and-biotechnology/articles/10.3389/fbioe.2025.1632806/full)). Our largest differences come from what the solver leaves out, so more iterations will not fix them, and optimising now would make the wrong answers faster. Nothing recommended here redraws a purchased pattern or adds a hardcoded fix; the proposed changes are to physics and measured values only.

## Fifteen differences, ranked by what shows on the garment

Terms used: **stretch** is size relative to the flat pattern, so 1.2 is 20% larger and 0.75 is 25% squeezed. The **block** is the base pattern. **Gathering** is our rule that fabric squeezed below flat size pushes back with no force, down to a limit of 50%. All first-hand results are on the `young` body, high cut, unless stated.

Evidence labels: **Tested** means measured on our real solver. **Published** means reported by a source, with its access limits stated. **Inferred** means hand calculation or reasoning from the audit notes, not tested. The ranking itself is my judgement: **no paper removes these factors one at a time on one garment** ([literature notes](../research_notes/Tight%20garment%20fit%20solver%20audit/skin_tight_papers.md)). Priority combines impact with how cheaply the question can be answered, so a low-impact row can still come early.

## Three first-hand tests correct the picture from the notes

**The starting shape does not set the overall fit on this garment.** The audit notes' simple tube test showed that with free compression and no friction, slack stays wherever the start left it, which raised the fear that our fit was an accident of its seed ([limits notes](../research_notes/Tight%20garment%20fit%20solver%20audit/solver_method_limits.md)). On the real garment, gathering fits started from the existing fit, a plain no-gathering fit, and 20 iterations of plain ended **0.4-0.6 mm apart on average, 95% of points within 1.4 mm, and 6.7-9.1 mm at worst**. Back-waist lengthwise stretch was 0.75-0.76 in all three, and the back leg edge's lowest point agreed within 0.4 mm. The tube result still holds as a warning for local placement, and only this family of starts has been tried. The 95% figure passes the project's gate of 95% of points within 3 mm, and the mean is close to the solver's own drift when simply left running (0.29 mm mean, 3.9 mm maximum over 600 more iterations, [refit page](../knowledge/engineering/app/refitting-after-an-edit.md#L24)), but the worst case is not.

**Earlier renders exaggerated the crotch, and that is now fixed.** The dev viewer placed fabric on the already-lifted surface and the shader added the lift again. The extra height at the crotch was **median 1.3-3.2 mm, 95% of points 5.7-9.2 mm, maximum 8.8-12.2 mm** across a-pose, lunge and passé; over the whole garment the median was 0 and the maximum 10.7-13.9 mm. Renders sent before the fix carried this error, so visual judgements of blocks made from them deserve a second look. From reading the code, stretch figures read the stored position rather than the shader's result and should be unaffected. With the error removed **the crotch nub is still visible, so the double lift is not its cause**. A 1.2 mm offset is still applied twice, which is minor.

**Re-fit accuracy measures agreement with ourselves, not with reality.** After a cutout, a whole-garment re-fit of 160 iterations (41-54 s) lands within 0.7-1.4 mm mean and 4.4-7.6 mm maximum of a 600-iteration answer, while keeping the old fit is off by 3.4-7.4 mm mean and 23-30 mm maximum, and a re-fit limited to near the hole stalls ([refit page](../knowledge/engineering/app/refitting-after-an-edit.md#L30)). These holes have rough edges and no elastic, on one body, so a finished cutout will differ. The numbers are a valid baseline for later speed work and say nothing about realism. The current fit page also records limits to keep in view: no pattern-maker review or sewn check, and drafted armhole and sleeve lines that still depart from the purchased block by up to 16.6 mm ([fit page](../knowledge/engineering/app/fitting-a-block-like-the-app.md#L96)). Those departures are a pattern matter and should not be blamed on the solver.

## Six choices are sound and should be left alone

The **free-compression force rule** is right in principle. Thin fabric buckles at about 0.1% of its working tension by my hand estimate, and tension field theory is an established way to predict coarse shape ([Chen 2021](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10333495)). Row 4 faults what happens to the spare fabric, not the force. Gathering is also not a tuning trick: the 15 mm the leg edge moves without it comes from charging compression as hard as stretch, which real fabric does not do. Below 0.5 the limit barely matters (5.2 mm lowest point at 0.5, 4.9 at 0.3), though no source supports that specific number, so triangles reaching it should be reported as too much fabric rather than a material limit.

The **solving method** is a recognised family: the published framework includes our rotation target and a version with bounded stretch ([Bouaziz 2014](https://users.cs.utah.edu/~ladislav/bouaziz14projective/bouaziz14projective.pdf)). On a rigid surface, overall fabric stiffness cancels out, so an unknown modulus does not by itself distort shape. Solving straight for a resting state, without motion, suits a standing fit, since public simulators also run to rest. **Flat pieces as the rest shape** match the better public tools ([Style3D's solver](https://github.com/newton-physics/newton/blob/main/newton/_src/solvers/style3d/cloth.py)) and keep the purchased pattern as the authority. **Welded seams**, the **front 1:1 and back 3/4 elastic ratios** (consistent with sewing guides, snippet only, [Craft and Fabric Links](https://craftandfabriclinks.com/~craftand/TIPS/elastic_instructions.html)), and **solving half and mirroring** (the body is symmetric to 0.52 mm at 95%, measured on `young`) need no work. Average stretch of 1.09-1.31 on drafted `young` ([drafted blocks](../knowledge/engineering/app/drafted-blocks.md#L54)), though measured before elastic and gathering, also sits in the plausible 10-40% range ([Montes 2020](https://users.encs.concordia.ca/~stpopa/res/img/publications_info/2020/Skintightclothing/Runway_SIGGRAPH2020_New.pdf)).

We also should not replace the solver with a public engine as the product. Those engines solve a harder problem: GarmentCodeData reports about 30 s on a strong GPU for full dressing and deliberately tunes for minimal stretch rather than stretch fit ([GarmentCodeData](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2405.17609)). Commercial tools are not a shortcut either, since one study found no correlation between virtual and real pressure in 32 cases (abstract only, [Vuruşkan and Ashdown 2024](https://gcris.ieu.edu.tr/entities/publication/807ba37d-13d3-4dbe-bfcd-3cd56a17c66e/full)). Licences also matter: GarmentCode's simulator fork is non-commercial ([licence note](https://github.com/maria-korosteleva/NvidiaWarp-GarmentCode)) and ARCSim is limited to educational, research and not-for-profit use ([licence](https://github.com/jiongchen/arcsim/blob/master/LICENSE)).

## Real fabric and a sewn garment must settle five questions

No amount of simulation can answer these, because every reference available, including a better simulator, is still a model. **Elastic and fabric strength** are the cheapest: a 250 mm loop of the real elastic and 50 mm strips of the real fabric in each direction, plus a seamed strip, hung with known weights of 100 to 1000 g and measured with a ruler, would beat anything public, since we found no force data for this elastic ([fabric notes](../research_notes/Tight%20garment%20fit%20solver%20audit/fabric_mechanics.md)). Measured values replace the guess in row 3 and supply rows 7 and 11. Halve the force for the loop, as two strands carry it. Repeat tests can vary by up to 20%, which sets how precise any comparison can claim to be ([Clyde 2017](https://www.math.ucdavis.edu/~jteran/papers/CTT17.pdf), woven fabric).

**Whether real leotards wrinkle at the small of the back, and where the leg line actually sits**, needs a sewn garment. We found no wrinkle map or strain map of a real worn tight garment anywhere. A grid printed on the garment and photographed on a dress form or adult wearer in several poses would test rows 1, 2, 4 and 8 at once, and would be stronger validation than any public project offers. Its limit is that the wearer will not match a digital body exactly, so it compares regional patterns of stretch rather than millimetres, unless the wearer is also scanned. **Friction** on nylon-spandex tricot has no direct measurement, and real fit depends on how the garment was pulled on, so a single right answer does not exist; any comparison needs a tolerance. **Flesh movement** at the crotch, seat and bust has no published millimetre figure. **Whether a worn garment sits on its loading or unloading force curve** could change working force by up to about half, based on knit data cycled far beyond wear strain, and is unknown at wear strain. A pattern maker's review would also check things no solver can.

## Measure first, then change physics, then optimise

Impact rank and working order differ, because some cheap checks decide what the expensive work should be. The order below also respects the standing rules: values come from measurement, never from matching the app's look, and no flat line changes.

| Step | Work | What it decides |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Add to the existing solver, with no physics change: a force-balance and energy measure, a ten-times-iterations run, a map of where the surface pulls fabric inward, and a map of triangles at the gathering limit. Report these as gathered, not fitted | Whether 240 iterations is enough. A small move per step says little: 0.019 mm could mean 0.019 mm or 19 mm remaining. Shows where rows 2, 4 and 10 are active |
| 2 | Owner sources real elastic, fabric and, when possible, a sewn sample. Hang-weight tests, as above | Replaces the estimated elastic strength and gives real grain figures |
| 3 | Make elastic strength per unit length rather than per mesh span, apply the measured value, then run 7, 5 and 3.5 mm meshes and a rotated grid against the 3 mm gate | Whether 7 mm is adequate. This cannot be read before the elastic fix, since refining the mesh now would silently change elastic strength |
| 4 | Run one garment in a heavier public solver as a reference, starting with libuipc's sewing sample or Newton's Style3D solver (both Apache-2.0), switching friction, one-sided contact, grain and narrowing on and off. libuipc needs CUDA, and the 4 GB GPU may limit mesh size (untested), so a rented GPU may be needed | Turns rows 2, 7, 8, 9 and 10 from inference into measured effects, which no paper has done. It measures model differences, not reality |
| 5 | Add the physics step 4 shows matters. Expected candidates: a surface that responds to the garment's own tension, and grain with narrowing | Fixes rows 2, 7 and 9 on evidence, in the order the measurements justify |
| 6 | Re-solve `back-arched` and `passe` from the standing fit, and compare positions and stretch with today's reuse | Sizes row 1 and says whether every pose needs a re-solve. Check lines and stretch too, since low millimetre error does not guarantee correct looks ([TailorNet](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2003.04583)) |
| 7 | Drive wrinkles from the solved state instead of fixed zones, with the owner's sign-off on the look | Fixes the visible half of row 4 |
| 8 | Printed-grid test on the sewn garment, ideally soon after step 5 | The only reality check. It can overturn earlier ranks |
| 9 | Speed optimisation, with the re-fit bench as baseline | Only now is the inner loop stable, and step 6 sets the real speed target |

## Conclusion

The audit reverses a natural assumption: the concern going in was hidden numerical error, such as too few iterations or a biased start, but on this garment the checks so far put it at about a millimetre for most points, with a proper force-balance check still to run. The larger risk is structural, because the fit's most visible results depend on values and surfaces chosen before any fabric was involved, and the solver can converge perfectly on them. That also means a good-looking fit is not yet evidence of realism, and tuning towards a good look would hide the very differences this audit found.

The most useful finding for planning is how cheap the next evidence is. A ruler, some weights, a printed grid and one reference run would turn most of this table from reasoned judgement into measurement, and would put the project ahead of the public work on the question that matters most here: whether a simulated stretch fit matches a real one.
